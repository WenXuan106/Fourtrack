How this actually works

This runs real signal processing in your browser, not a trained separation model — no AI, which is why it's free with no server involved. For every moment and every frequency in the track, it compares the left and right channels: content that matches closely between them — usually the lead vocal, mixed dead-center — gets pulled into Vocals; everything left over becomes Instrumental, and it's also checked against the frequency range a voice actually occupies so a dead-center kick drum or bass line doesn't get mistaken for vocal. That's a frequency-by-frequency, moment-by-moment comparison (a short-time Fourier transform under the hood), not one blanket subtraction across the whole mix. Bass and Other are then a clean low/high split of the instrumental at 180 Hz. The separation strength slider controls how strict the left/right match has to be — push it up when vocals are still audible in Instrumental or Other, since a vocal with studio widening or doubling on it won't match perfectly between channels and needs more leniency to fully capture. That comes at the cost of a slightly less pure Vocals stem. Some vocals are widened enough that even at max strength a little will remain in the mix — that's the real ceiling of a technique based on stereo position rather than trained recognition of what a voice sounds like. It still won't cleanly lift drums out of a dense mix the way a paid neural separator will, but for isolating vocals, building karaoke tracks, and pulling out a bassline, it's well beyond a simple phase-cancellation trick — and your audio never leaves this tab.

This mode runs HTDemucs, an open-source neural network trained specifically to recognize what drums, bass, vocals, and other instruments actually sound like — not just where they sit in the stereo field. That's why it can cleanly split out Drums as its own stem, something the built-in mode can't do at all. The first time you use this mode, your browser downloads the model weights (~170MB, one time, from Hugging Face's public model hub) and caches them locally. After that, everything — loading the model and running it on your track — happens entirely inside this tab using ONNX Runtime Web and your device's GPU or CPU. Your audio file itself is never uploaded anywhere, at any point — only the (public, generic) model weights are fetched, the same file for every user. The real cost is memory: the model plus four full-length stems held in the browser's tab can add up to more than a phone or tablet is willing to give a single web page, and unlike a crash from bad code, there's no clean way to catch that from inside the page — the tab just gets killed. It needs a reasonably modern desktop browser to be reliable; treat mobile as a "might work on a short clip" situation, not a guarantee.

This mode sends your audio file to a server you deploy and control yourself, which runs the same real Demucs model and sends back four separated WAV stems. It exists for one reason: some devices (iPad and iPhone Safari especially) can't reliably run the AI mode's ~170MB model in-browser without risking a tab crash from hitting the OS memory ceiling — running it server-side sidesteps that entirely, at the honest cost of your audio leaving the device. It only goes to whatever URL you've entered above — your own deployment, not a service run by Anthropic or anyone else. The backend's README covers three ways to host it: a free Render web service (real, but a tight memory fit for this model), Hugging Face Spaces (reliably fits, now requires their $9/mo PRO plan), or on your own computer over your home WiFi (fully free, but only reachable on that network).

Runs fully client-side. No account, no upload, no server for your audio — ever.
